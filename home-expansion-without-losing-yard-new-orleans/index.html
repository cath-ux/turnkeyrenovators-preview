How to Expand a New Orleans Home Without Losing the Yard

By TurnKey RenovatorsPublished August 31, 2026Updated September 4, 20267 min read

On a New Orleans lot, the question is never just “can we add a room.” It is “where does the room go, what does the city allow there, and what happens to the yard when it is done.”

Many New Orleans lots are long and narrow, the house already sits close to the sidewalk, and the only open ground is out back. So when a family outgrows the house, the obvious move, building straight back into the yard, is also the one that costs them the outdoor space they were counting on. Here at TurnKey Renovators, we see this trade-off often. Let’s break down the ways to add real square footage here and what each one does to the lot.

If you are already sketching an addition and want to know what your lot will actually let you build, contact us and we will look at the lot with you before drawings start.

What’s Actually Constraining Your Lot?

Before talking about what to build, it helps to know what is already decided for you. Three things set the boundaries on a New Orleans expansion, and none of them are the size of your budget.

The Setbacks, by the Numbers

In the historic urban neighborhood zoning districts that cover much of the city, the zoning ordinance’s bulk table sets the rear yard at 20 percent of the lot depth or 15 feet, whichever is less, for the two-family districts. The single-family district uses 20 feet in place of 15. Interior side yards are 3 feet in the two-family districts, and 3 feet or 10 percent of the lot width, whichever is greater, on single-family lots. Building height tops out at 35 feet, or 40 feet in the Garden District.

Those numbers are more generous than many owners expect. On a 30-by-120-foot lot, for example, the required rear yard is 15 feet, not half the yard. Your own district and lot dimensions decide the exact figures, so look up your zoning district on the city’s online zoning map before anything else. The real limit is usually the next item.

The 30 Percent Permeable Open Space Rule

The same table requires a minimum of 30 percent of the lot area to stay permeable, meaning ground that water can soak into. In the Garden District that rises to 40 percent on lots wider than 40 feet. This rule is the one that quietly decides how big an addition can be, because every square foot of new footprint, plus every square foot of new concrete patio, counts against it.

So an addition that fits inside the setbacks can still fail the open space check, and the fix is often not shrinking the addition but rethinking the paving around it.

The Historic District Review (If You’re In One)

If your house sits in a local historic district, the Historic District Landmarks Commission reviews any addition that is visible from the public right-of-way. Its own guidelines say additions at the rear are generally the most appropriate, side additions are rarely appropriate and should be held back as far as possible from the street, and additions on the front elevation are generally inappropriate. In practice that pushes the expansion toward the back, which is exactly where the yard lives. Verify your district and its current rules at the start, not after drawings exist.

Okay, So Where Does the Room Go?

Here is the thing: “back” is not the only direction. New Orleans has been solving this problem for generations, and the answers are already standing on most blocks.

Go Up at the Back (The Camelback)

The camelback is the city’s original answer to a full lot. It is a second story added over the rear rooms of a one-story house, so the street-facing facade keeps its original scale while the back gains a whole floor. The HDLC guidelines describe camelbacks as traditionally designed additions on wood-frame shotgun and double shotgun houses, note that the second story was traditionally set back at least two rooms from the front, and treat them more leniently than rooftop additions as long as they stay compatible with the existing house.

For the yard, this is the best case. The footprint does not grow at all. The trade is structural: the existing rear walls and foundation have to carry a second floor, which is a real engineering question on an older frame house and the first thing we check.

Build Back, But Narrower

If the addition has to go on the ground, it does not have to be the full width of the house. A rear addition held to one side, with the other side left open as a covered porch or a planted strip, keeps a usable run of yard along the house and keeps more ground permeable. It also reads as a secondary wing rather than a bigger box, which is what the historic guidelines are asking for anyway.

The 3-foot side yard is the number to design around here. A wing that holds 3 feet off one property line and leaves 8 to 10 feet open on the other side keeps a real walkway to the back, keeps a window wall on the addition, and keeps the neighbor’s light. The full-width box does none of those things, and it is the version most people sketch first.

Reclaim the Ground You Are Not Using

Many New Orleans lots already carry a detached shed, a dead concrete pad, or a side yard too narrow to do anything with. Removing a failing outbuilding or old paving can hand back permeable area and, on the right lot, make room for an addition that would otherwise fail the open space check. It is the least glamorous option and can be the cheapest square footage on the site, and it is one of the first things we look for on a lot survey before anyone talks about building bigger.

The Yard Is Part of the Plan (Not the Leftover)

Whatever direction the addition goes, the yard should be designed at the same time, not whatever is left when construction stops. Two reasons.

First, the open space rule means paving decisions and building decisions are the same decision. Permeable pavers, a planted strip along a fence, or a smaller patio can be the difference between an addition that passes and one that does not.

Second, the yard you keep should be the yard you use. A deep, shaded rear yard that connects to the addition through a covered outdoor living space is worth more than a wider strip of grass nobody sits in. We covered the weather side of that in our post on planning an outdoor kitchen for New Orleans weather, and the same logic applies to any covered space off an addition.

The addition that “loses the yard” is usually the one that was designed first and left the yard to chance.

What About Flood Rules?

This is the question people forget until the permit stage. Under the National Flood Insurance Program rules that participating communities adopt into their floodplain ordinances, an addition or other improvement whose cost equals or exceeds 50 percent of the building’s market value before construction is a substantial improvement, and the building then has to meet the requirements for new construction, including elevation. Additions are specifically counted as improvements that increase the square footage of a structure, and some communities add up improvements over several years rather than per project, so phasing a project is not a way around the rule.

What that means on the ground: a large addition on a house that sits below the base flood elevation can trigger a requirement to raise the whole house, not just the new part. That changes the budget and the design, so the market value, the flood zone, and the city’s current floodplain ordinance need to be on the table at the first meeting. A camelback or a more modest addition is sometimes the answer to that math, not just to the yard.

Bottom Line: Design the Lot, Not Just the Room

Expanding a New Orleans home without losing the yard comes down to three moves. Know the setbacks, the 30 percent open space rule, and whether the historic commission has a say. Look up before looking back, because the camelback was built for exactly this problem. And design the yard and the paving with the addition, so the open ground you keep is ground you will actually use.
